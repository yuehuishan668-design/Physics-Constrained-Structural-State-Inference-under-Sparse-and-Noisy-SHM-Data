# 1. Introduction

Structural health monitoring (SHM) provides an important technical basis for condition assessment, damage detection, and maintenance decision-making in civil infrastructure. By collecting structural response data from sensors, SHM systems can support continuous or periodic evaluation of structural performance. However, transforming monitoring data into reliable structural state information remains challenging, especially when the available data are sparse, noisy, and limited in labeled damage observations.

In practical monitoring scenarios, sensor deployment is often constrained by cost, accessibility, installation conditions, long-term maintenance, and data transmission requirements. As a result, only a limited number of structural response channels may be available. In addition, measured responses are affected by noise, environmental variability, operational uncertainty, and excitation differences. These factors make structural damage inference difficult because response changes may be caused by damage, input variation, measurement uncertainty, or their combined effects.

Data-driven methods have been widely explored for SHM-related damage identification and structural state inference. With sufficient labeled data, machine learning and deep learning models can approximate complex relationships between structural responses and damage states. However, in many civil infrastructure applications, labeled damage data are limited, severe damage observations are rare, and field measurements are noisy and incomplete. Under such conditions, purely data-driven models may show unstable generalization, limited interpretability, and unreliable behavior in safety-critical damage regimes.

A key issue is that average prediction accuracy alone may not be sufficient for SHM evaluation. Metrics such as mean absolute error (MAE) and root mean squared error (RMSE) summarize global prediction performance, but they can hide systematic errors in severe damage cases. For structural safety assessment, underestimating severe damage is more critical than producing small errors in low-damage or healthy cases. Therefore, SHM-oriented damage inference should be evaluated not only by average accuracy, but also by damage-stratified reliability, prediction bias, and underestimation behavior in high-damage regimes.

Another issue is the role of feature representation. Increasing estimator complexity does not necessarily improve reliability when the input representation does not encode physically meaningful structural information. Raw response histories can contain useful information, but they may also include noise, excitation effects, and redundant temporal variations. Physics-informed descriptor construction provides a practical alternative: instead of relying entirely on end-to-end representation learning, structural responses can be transformed into physically motivated descriptors that summarize response magnitude, frequency characteristics, spatial response patterns, inter-channel correlations, and excitation-related information.

In this study, a physics-informed descriptor construction framework is developed and evaluated for structural damage inference under sparse and noisy SHM-like data. A controlled OpenSeesPy-based simulation workflow is used to generate structural response data with story-level damage labels. A 92-dimensional descriptor set is constructed from response-derived and excitation-related features. The framework is then evaluated through main feature ablation, repeated-split robustness, damage-stratified reliability diagnosis, noise robustness analysis, and sensor sparsity stress testing.

The purpose of this study is not to claim direct field deployment readiness, nor to replace field validation. Instead, the objective is to provide a controlled and reproducible evaluation of whether physics-informed descriptor construction can support stable damage inference under sparse and noisy monitoring conditions, while also identifying reliability boundaries that may be hidden by average metrics.

The main contributions of this study are summarized as follows.

1. A physics-informed descriptor construction framework is developed for structural damage inference using sparse and noisy SHM-like response data. The full descriptor set integrates response statistics, frequency-domain indicators, spatial response descriptors, response correlation descriptors, and excitation-related metadata.

2. A controlled OpenSeesPy-based simulation workflow is established to generate a 3000-case structural response dataset with four-story damage labels. The workflow supports systematic evaluation of descriptor construction, estimator selection, noise robustness, and sensor sparsity.

3. A comprehensive experimental protocol is conducted, including main feature ablation, repeated-split robustness, damage-stratified reliability diagnosis, noise robustness analysis, and sensor sparsity stress testing. This protocol evaluates not only average prediction accuracy but also robustness and reliability under safety-relevant conditions.

4. The results show that the full physics-informed descriptor set provides the strongest overall representation. In the main four-sensor setting, ridge regression with the full descriptor set achieves the best fixed-split performance and remains stable across repeated train/validation/test partitions and tested noise levels.

5. The study demonstrates that average accuracy alone is insufficient for SHM-oriented damage inference. Damage-stratified analysis reveals systematic high-damage underestimation, and sensor sparsity stress testing identifies severe sensor reduction as a practical reliability boundary.
